Reasoning¶

The reasoner works out what follows from a model: what’s inherited, what’s implied by relations’ properties, what rules and definitions conclude – and what’s wrong. It uses clingo, an answer set programming system – or, if you choose it, Nemo, a Datalog engine that can also say why a conclusion holds, CLIPS, a production rule system, or SWI-Prolog (see Engines and explanations below).

Reasoning¶

Reason (▶, on the reasoning toolbar along the bottom of the window, or reasoning | reason on the background menu) reasons over the window’s model; Retract (↺) takes what it inferred back out again. Reason silently (▷, or reasoning | reason silently) reasons too, but doesn’t add what it infers to the views – only the problems are marked, and only a summary is printed: eg: so that queries find what the rules conclude without cluttering the canvas. (Hover over a button to see what it does.) At the toolbar’s left, a small box says which reasoner engine is used (see Engines and explanations): click it to choose another, from a menu of the engines. The toolbar also says what the last reason found – how many relations and new objects it inferred, and how many problems – and, at its right, whether the model can be edited: a pencil, crossed out in red if it can’t be here. Click the pencil (or choose Edit | Lock view) to lock the view, so it can’t change the model – not even by dragging the end of an arc to another object – but can still be arranged (moving things, erasing them from view, layouts) and saved; do it again to unlock it. The lock is saved with the view, and other views of the model aren’t locked. A read-only model’s view (eg: the system model, or the base rules) can’t be unlocked. (View | Reasoning toolbar hides it, or shows it again.) The report goes to the terminal pane, in sections:

Violations

Errors and warnings: relations whose source or destination is outside their type’s domain or range, isa cycles, an object that isa an individual, redundant parents, irreflexive or asymmetric relations broken, relation types tagged with conflicting properties, and rules the reasoner can’t use.

Inferred relations

Relations implied by the relational properties (symmetric, transitive, …) of their types, and by nodes their links define – and, on the clingo engine, what the cases of those definitions conclude (see Defining links).

Rule conclusions

What the rules and definitions concluded: relations, attribute values, types, and new objects (see Contexts: Rules, Definitions, Queries, Constraints and Requirements).

Inherited relations

Relations an individual has because one of its types has them.

Suggested classifications

Types an object could be given to make a relation it’s in fit its type’s domain or range.

The results are also shown on the canvas, in every view of the model:

  • inferred relations (and the relations and types rules conclude) are added to the model as inferred relations, drawn in their usual look, but faded, with dashed lines; inherited relations the same, with dotted lines. They’re relations like any other – they take part in the layouts, for example – but they aren’t saved with the model or the view, and adding them doesn’t count as a change to either (even a read-only model can be reasoned over). They’re the reasoner’s: they can’t be edited, removed, or related to anything – but mark as asserted in an inferred relation’s menu makes it an ordinary (asserted) part of the model, saved with it (not in a read-only model). It can be undone;

  • attribute values rules conclude for the model’s own objects (eg: from a definition’s constraints) are added as inferred attributes: they’re used like any other value, but aren’t saved, and an object’s own (given) value always wins over one inferred. If rules conclude different values of one attribute for an object, it gets the first rule’s (in the order they’re in the model, and then its super-models), whichever engine reasoned. The attribute editor shows an inferred value greyed out, marked I, with an assert button: press it, then Save Changes, to make it an ordinary value;

  • objects with violations get a red E marker: hover over it for the problems, or choose show error report from the object’s menu.

Inferred relations are shown only between objects that are both shown in the view.

The results stay until you reason again or choose reasoning | clear reasoning – they aren’t updated as you edit, so reason again after changing the model. Reasoning again keeps the inferred relations that are still inferred (where you’ve put them), adds new ones, and removes the rest. Typing reason (or clear reasoning) in the terminal does the same as the button.

Reasoning about some objects¶

An object’s menu has reason, reason silently and retract too: they’re about just that object – or, if it’s selected, everything selected.

  • reason adds (and draws) what the reasoner concludes about it, and marks its problems; the report in the terminal pane is about it too. What’s about an object is what it takes part in: the relations inferred to or from it, the types and attribute values the rules conclude for it, the new objects a rule makes for it (with their types and relations), and the violations that name it. For a relation, it’s also what’s inferred between its two ends (eg: for bob has-sibling alice: alice has-sibling bob); for a relation type, the relations of that type; for a rule, what it concludes.

  • reason silently adds the same to the model, without drawing it, and marks its problems.

  • retract takes back what’s about it – even what reasoning about something else brought in (eg: a relation to it) – and its problems’ markers. The rest stays.

The reasoner still reasons over the whole model – what holds of one object can depend on any of it: eg: carol has-uncle bob follows from carol has-parent alice and alice has-brother bob, which is itself inferred – it’s just what’s added that’s chosen. Each one adds to what’s there (where it still holds: what’s no longer concluded goes), and the last say wins: an object reasoned about after another was retracted gets back what’s between them. The reasoning toolbar’s summary says what’s shown: eg, “about carol and bob: inferred: 3 relations; 1 warning”. A whole reason shows everything again; Retract (↺) and clear reasoning take everything back. In the terminal: reason carol bob (--silent too), and retract carol.

Engines and explanations¶

The reasoner has four engines, which reach the same conclusions: clingo (the default), Nemo, CLIPS and Prolog (SWI-Prolog). The apps include clingo and Nemo; CLIPS and Prolog are installed separately (see Installing an engine, which also covers the .pyz and the source). The reasoner/engine setting (Settings…, in the application menu, or the box at the left of the reasoning toolbar) says which one reason, queries and ask reasoner use. (In the box’s menu, an engine that isn’t installed isn’t chosen: it says how to install it instead.) reason --engine nemo picks one for a single run. ask reasoner takes a question in the engine’s own language: for clingo, 'ans(X) :- holds(T,F,X), name(T,"owns"), name(F,"fred").'; for Nemo, 'ans(?X) :- holds(?T,?F,?X), name(?T,"owns"), name(?F,"fred").'; for CLIPS, '(defrule q (holds ?t ?f ?x) (name ?t "owns") (name ?f "fred") => (assert (ans ?x)))'; for Prolog, 'ans(X) :- name(T,"owns"), name(F,"fred"), holds(T,F,X).' (its goals run in the order they’re written, so start with what narrows things down most). Only clingo explores the cases of definitions (see Defining links) and finds a KNet net’s probes (see Plugins).

What Nemo adds is explanations. An inferred relation’s menu has explain (why it holds), which prints the steps to the terminal pane: the rules it follows by (yours, and the base rules: eg, transitive), down to what the model asserts. Eg:

ann -ancestorOf-> fred
  ann -ancestorOf-> fred, by the rule "transitive"
    ann -ancestorOf-> mary (asserted)
    mary -ancestorOf-> fred (asserted)
    ancestorOf is transitive

The terminal’s explain does the same for any relation, or any type and two objects: explain ancestorOf ann fred (--full for every step the engine took). It uses Nemo whichever engine reasons.

One difference: in a rule (not a constraint or a query), Nemo and CLIPS test a not attribute constraint (eg: not gender: female) against the attributes the model gives, not those other rules conclude – they need the rules in an order they can work through, one layer at a time.

Installing an engine¶

When an engine that isn’t installed is wanted – reason with the reasoner/engine setting on it, explain (why it holds), or the terminal’s explain – the program says so, with a link to here. If it’s the one the setting picks, it also offers to reason with one that is installed: this time, or from now on.

In the apps

clingo and Nemo are in them. An engine an app doesn’t include – CLIPS or Prolog – can be installed for a Python 3.14 on the computer – the same version as the app’s own, for the same kind of processor (eg: Apple silicon) – and the app uses it from there:

python3.14 -m pip install clipspy

(on Windows, py -3.14 -m pip install clipspy). For Prolog, first install SWI-Prolog itself (9.1.12 or later: on a Mac, its app, or brew install swi-prolog; on Windows, its installer, letting it add swipl to the PATH; on Linux, its PPA or your distribution’s package), then its Python interface:

python3.14 -m pip install janus-swi

(pip builds it against the swipl it finds on the PATH, so open a new terminal after installing SWI-Prolog. The Mac app’s swipl isn’t on the PATH: put it there for pip with PATH=/Applications/SWI-Prolog.app/Contents/MacOS:$PATH python3.14 -m pip install janus-swi.) The program finds SWI-Prolog’s files itself when it reasons, even when swipl isn’t on the PATH; if it can’t, set the SWI_HOME_DIR environment variable to the folder that swipl --dump-runtime-variables gives as PLBASE. If there’s no Python 3.14 on the computer yet, install one from https://www.python.org first. The app looks for that Python where its installers put it (python.org’s, Homebrew’s, …) and on the PATH; if it’s somewhere else (eg: a virtual environment), give its executable as the reasoner/python setting (eg: /Users/me/venvs/sn/bin/python).

The .pyz or the source

Install the engine for the Python that runs the program (see Getting Started): clingo with python3.14 -m pip install clingo, Nemo with python3.14 -m pip install nemo-python, CLIPS with:

python3.14 -m pip install clipspy

and Prolog, once SWI-Prolog is installed (see above), with:

python3.14 -m pip install janus-swi

Then start the program again.

Switching rules off¶

To try a model without one of its rules, definitions, constraints or requirements, disable it: choose disable from its menu (or give it a disabled attribute that’s true – every context has one, false to start with). The reasoner ignores it, and it’s drawn with a red slash through it. enable (or undo) puts it back. Reason again to see the difference.

Highlights¶

answer query and show conclusions (see Contexts: Rules, Definitions, Queries, Constraints and Requirements) highlight the objects they found. reasoning | clear highlights – or Esc – clears them.

Tests and conclusions¶

It’s worth keeping the two roles of a pattern straight:

  • a query, a rule’s antecedent, and either side of a definition when it’s being matched, only test the model: nothing in them is asserted;

  • a rule’s consequent (and a definition’s other side) is concluded for each match: its relations, its variables’ types and its variables’ constraints all become conclusions.

The conclusions are added to the model, but as inferred objects and attributes (see above): faded, not saved, not editable, taken away by Retract (and by the next reason, once they’re no longer concluded – those still concluded are kept). The model still says only what you drew, until you mark as asserted the ones you want to keep.

Validation¶

model | validate (Option-V) is a quicker check, done without the reasoner: it checks each object’s own consistency (its attributes, its types, its relations’ ends). Its problems are shown the same way, with E markers.
