The Reasoner¶

The reasoner works out what follows from a model. It’s in two layers:

sn.model.reasoner

The engine-independent part: the Reasoner interface, the export of a model as facts, the reading of rules, definitions and queries into patterns, and what an engine returns (Inferences, Violation).

sn.model.clingoReasoner

The engine: ClingoReasoner, built on clingo, an answer set programming (ASP) system, with its rule base in clingoReasoner.lp.

Engines register themselves by name (class ClingoReasoner(Reasoner, engine="clingo")) and are made with Reasoner.create(model, engine). Another engine (eg: a description logic reasoner) would implement the same interface; test/test_model/test_reasoner.py pins down the semantics every engine must share.

Why ASP¶

A semantic network’s meaning – inheritance, relational properties, signatures, rules – is naturally a set of logic rules over its structure, and clingo evaluates such rules directly, to a fixed point, with negation (for “unless a more specific relation says otherwise”). The rules are short, declarative, and kept in one file (clingoReasoner.lp), separate from the Python.

To keep relation types first-class – in the isa hierarchy, and themselves related – while staying first-order, every GraphObject is reified as a term o(N): “fred likes mary” is link(o(31), o(21), o(23)) together with parent(o(31), o(12)) (the relation is a likes), not likes(fred, mary).

The pipeline¶

  1. facts() exports the model as engine-neutral facts (their vocabulary is listed in the module’s documentation). Pattern elements – the contents of rules and queries – are exported too, but marked pattern, so the rule base doesn’t take them as facts about the world.

  2. The user’s rules and definitions are compiled to clingo rules (below).

  3. clingo grounds and solves the facts, the compiled rules and the rule base together. The program is stratified (no recursion through negation), so there’s exactly one answer set, and it’s found without search.

  4. The atoms shown (#show) are mapped back to GraphObjects, as an Inferences.

sn.model.reasoner.reason() runs this, keeps the result on the model (model.reasoning), and notifies the model’s observers with a ("reasoned", (inferences,)) topic: every view draws the results. They’re a snapshot – not saved, and not updated as the model is edited – until the next reason() or clearReasoning().

What was inferred is also put in the model, as inferred objects (GraphObject.inferred): materialize() makes a relation for each inferred or inherited relation, an isa link for each one the rules concluded, and a node for each object the rules concluded exists (NewObject) – named for the rule’s variable and the match, eg: “(m for alice)” – with the types, attributes and relations the rules gave it. Each one’s inferredKind says what it is (“inferred”, “inherited”, “rule” or “new”); a new object is kept from one “reason” to the next by its rule, variable and match. So the model’s own queries – getRelatedTo(), isa, inherited attributes – see them like anything else. They’re kept apart from what’s asserted:

  • they’re not saved (the model’s object table leaves them out), and adding or removing them doesn’t make the model dirty, or go on the undo list – even a write-locked model can be reasoned over (see Model.inferring());

  • the reasoner doesn’t read them: while it gathers its facts they’re taken out of the model and put back afterwards (withoutInferences()) – otherwise an inferred isa link, say, would give its object the type’s attributes, and justify itself;

  • the next reason() keeps those still inferred (the same objects), adds the new ones, and removes the rest; clearReasoning() removes them all;

  • they’re the reasoner’s: changing one’s attributes raises PermissionError (GraphObject._refuseIfInferred()), and the model commands refuse to remove, reconnect or relate them, or make instances of them – until the mark asserted command (ModelContext.mark_asserted(), undoable; not in a write-locked model) makes one asserted, with what has to come with it (GraphObject.assertedWith(): its inferred isa links, a relation’s inferred ends, and the relations a rule gave a new object – without them the next reason() would make another in its place).

Attribute values the rules conclude for the model’s own objects (ruleAttrs) become inferred attributes (Attribute.inferred, in pythonUtils): set where the object has no value of its own (nor that value inherited), not saved with it, taken out while the reasoner gathers its facts, and kept, updated or removed by the next reason(). Setting a value where one is inferred replaces it with an ordinary one (GraphObject.__setitem__), so undo brings the inferred one back; the attribute editor’s assert button does the same.

Views show the new objects related to objects they show (beside them), and the inferred relations between objects they show (SNGView._showInferred(), on the "reasoned" topic) as ordinary view objects that are drawn faded (VObject.isInferred(); the vis cache fades their colours toward the background) with dashed lines – dotted for inherited ones (the relation’s inferredKind). They take part in layouts like anything else, but aren’t saved with the view, and neither they nor the model’s inferred objects count when a view or model checks whether it’s dirty.

The rule base¶

clingoReasoner.lp defines, over the facts:

  • isa_star: the reflexive-transitive isa closure (including types that rules conclude);

  • holds(T,S,D): S is related to D under relation type T – directly, by a subtype of T, by inheritance down the isa hierarchy, or by a rule (including the base rules: see below);

  • inferred and inherited: the holds that weren’t asserted (and aren’t implied by a more specific relation type);

  • classify(X,C): a type that would make a relation’s end fit its signature;

  • violation(Code, Severity, Subject, Args): the problems, whose codes are turned into messages by reasoner._MESSAGES.

Rules and definitions¶

patternOf() reads a context (or some of its panes) into a Pattern: its variables, constants, relations, isa links and constraints. ruleOf() reads a rule into a RuleSpec: the antecedent’s pattern, and the consequent’s conclusions – relations (heads), attribute values (assertions), types (isas), and the variables that only the consequent has (new: objects to create). A definition is two RuleSpecs, one each way (definitionSpecs()). Problems found on the way (eg: a consequent relation with no end) are recorded in the spec, and reported instead of compiling the rule.

ClingoReasoner._rulesProgram then compiles each spec to one clingo rule per conclusion, the antecedent as the body (_patternBody):

fired(Rule, T, S, D)        :- <antecedent>.    % a relation
rule_attr(Rule, X, Key, V)  :- <antecedent>.    % an attribute value
rule_isa(Rule, X, Type)     :- <antecedent>.    % a type

and the rule base feeds these back in, as holds, attr and isa. So rules chain: one rule’s conclusions can satisfy another’s antecedent.

New objects¶

A consequent-only variable stands for an object that exists for each match. It’s represented by a term that’s a function of the match – new(Rule, Name, ((Var, Value), ...)) – so there’s exactly one per match, and reported as a NewObject. Two things keep this finite and stratified:

  • the variables of a rule that creates objects range over the model’s data only, never over created objects (base/individual rather than data/any_individual), so it can’t keep creating objects from its own creations (a rule that creates nothing can’t run away, so its variables range over all the data, created objects included);

  • an object is created only if the data doesn’t already have one doing the job (_satisfied) – tested against asserted relations and types only, so the test doesn’t depend on its own outcome.

Cardinality¶

A relation type’s cardinality and inverseCardinality attributes (CARDINALITY, read by parseCardinality()) are exported as card(T, out|in, Min, Max) facts (Max -1 for any number), and the rule base (clingoReasoner.lp, “Cardinality”) gives them their meaning:

  • Merging (at most one): same(X, Y) for two objects related to (or from) the same object, one of them new – and its symmetric and transitive closure. What’s true of one is then true of the other (holds, attr and isa_star are copied across): positive rules, so the program stays stratified. Two of the model’s objects are never merged (they have different names): their being the same is reported, as too many. mergeSame() then replaces each new object by what stands for its class (sameClasses(): the model’s object, if there’s one, else one of the new ones) in the results, and the engine’s answer() does the same for answers.

  • Inventing (exactly one): a new(T, Name, (("of", X),)) object ("to" for in) for each of the domain’s (range’s) individuals with no asserted relation by T – like a rule’s new objects, only for the model’s own individuals (base), and tested against asserted relations only.

  • Checking: cardinality_max (_in) counts the model’s objects only (a new one might be any of them); cardinality_min (_in) counts new ones too, once per merged class.

The relation triples in the results are sorted into the models’ order (see Reasoner._finish()), so they don’t depend on the engine’s.

Constraints¶

constraintOf() reads a constraint into a ConstraintSpec: its pattern, and from its attributes the violation’s code, severity, what it reports (subject first) and message. Each compiles to one clingo rule:

violation0(Code, Severity, Subject, (Args,)) :- <pattern>.

A requirement (a constraint that isa requirement) has a second pattern, its required part (required); its pattern is its condition. It compiles to two rules, over the condition’s variables (Vars):

requirement_holds(Req, (Vars,)) :- <condition>, <required>.
violation0(Code, Severity, Subject, (Args,)) :- <condition>, not requirement_holds(Req, (Vars,)).

The first has the condition in its body too, so the variables the two parts share have values even where the required part only uses them in a not (eg: not gender: ?g); its own variables are existential.

_finish() – which every engine passes its results through – reports a violation once per set of objects it’s about (so a constraint matching a-b and b-a reports it once), and gives a constraint’s violations its message.

The base rules¶

The meaning of the relational properties (symmetric, transitive, reflexive, irreflexive, asymmetric) isn’t in clingoReasoner.lp: it’s ordinary rules and constraints, in a model of base rules (sn/model/baseRules.snm, made by tools/makeBaseRules.py) that every reasoner applies to every model (ruleObjects()), so a new engine gets them by translating rules, with nothing more to write. They use a relation-type variable (^r) tagged with the property, and individual (?a) or “any” (!a) variables by what a relation with a type at an end means – “every” at its source, “some” at its destination: eg: !a -^r-> ?b with ^r {symmetric} concludes ?b -^r-> !a (but nothing from a -r-> T: which T?); transitivity is all !; reflexive, irreflexive and asymmetric are all ?. See tools/makeBaseRules.py.

Variable names¶

Which names are variables, and of which kind, is decided in one place: sn.model.names.NameGrammar.parse() (?x individual, ^T type, !a any; anything else a constant – and a variable whose stem starts with _, eg: ?_b, is hidden: reportedAnswers() leaves it out of a query’s answers). Everything else asks it, through variableKind() or sn.model.names.isVariableName() – the engines (a variable’s kind is a guard in clingo: individual(V), type(V) or none), the constraints’ value variables, the views. An application that needs its own syntax for names subclasses it and installs it with setGrammar(); it should leave the names it doesn’t claim to the default (super().parse()), as the base rules are written in it.

BASE_RULES lists the base rules’ models, and an application can change it (eg: to base rules of its own), before it makes or reads a model. baseRuleModels() reads them (write-locked) once. Like the system model, they’re super-models of every model (implicitSuperDBs()): they can’t be removed, and aren’t saved with it (the file read may be a temporary copy of package data), but are added again when it’s read. objects() leaves them out: their objects aren’t facts, and their conclusions are reported as plain inferences, unless the reasoner/showSysRules preference (or the reasoner’s showSysRules) is on.

Queries¶

answer() compiles a query’s pattern to an ans(...) rule, solves it with the model’s facts and the results of reasoning (a query’s variables do range over created objects), and returns one dict per match: variable to object. query() takes clingo rules defining ans directly (the terminal’s ask reasoner).
